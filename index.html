The Protocol

Examine the passage from evidence to action.

Information moves from source to representation, from representation to inference, and from inference to action. At each step, a claim can acquire certainty or authority that the original material never supplied.

The Decision Integrity Protocol examines that passage. It begins by distinguishing the decision actually being made from the story being told about it.

The examination asks:

  1. What decision is actually being made?
  2. What is established, and what is being assumed?
  3. What information is missing, uncertain, or being discounted?
  4. What pressures, incentives, loyalties, fears, or habits may be influencing judgment?
  5. Who bears the consequences of action, inaction, delay, or error?
  6. What values or obligations are being protected, compromised, or left unspoken?
  7. What evidence or circumstance would require reconsideration?
  8. Can the reasoning be explained honestly to those affected?

The protocol does not manufacture certainty. It does not turn an unknown into a fact, an interpretation into an authorization, or a plausible answer into sufficient grounds for action.

Its purpose is to make the reasoning, boundaries, and consequences of a decision examinable.

What the examination follows

Five connected dimensions organize the work:

  • Source and object continuity. Is the material still about the same object, record, person, transaction, or decision? Has a later representation quietly replaced the original?
  • Conditions and qualifiers. Have the limitations, exceptions, uncertainties, and conditions survived interpretation and transmission?
  • Authority and permitted action. Does the evidence establish that the relevant actor has authority to make the statement or perform the proposed action?
  • Evidence and change. What new evidence has been introduced? What does it resolve, and what remains unknown?
  • Continuity and accountability. Can the reasoning be traced across successive representations and decisions without silently rewriting the earlier record?

These are connected questions, not independent scores. No single favorable answer substitutes for the examination as a whole.

When the record does not support the proposed action, the examination identifies the boundary: what the evidence permits, what it does not, and what would need to be established before proceeding.

The Named Failures

Four ways a claim acquires force it has not earned.

01

Coherent Substitution

The answer is coherent. It answers a different question.

An agent is asked whether a termination notice satisfies a specific contractual condition. It explains whether termination is legally possible without examining whether the notice meets that condition.

02

Object Drift

The label survives. The object changes.

An examination concerns a signed agreement. A later summary or revised document is silently substituted, and the conclusion is still attributed to the original.

03

Qualifier Stripping

A condition disappears, and the conclusion changes with it.

“May reduce risk” becomes “reduces risk.”

04

Authority Leakage

Information acquires authority it was never given.

An AI-generated summary of a signed agreement is repeated until another system treats the summary itself as the agreement.

The named failures describe what can go wrong. The examination determines what the submitted record establishes in the particular case.

Coherent Substitution

Proposition integrity beyond answering the wrong question.

“A system can fail before its reasoning becomes wrong. It can replace the object it was asked to evaluate, reason coherently about the replacement, and present the result as though it answered the original.”

A response may be logical, detailed, and sophisticated while still completely failing the assigned task. The defining error is not disagreement, factual inaccuracy, or weak reasoning. It is an unauthorized change in the proposition before evaluation begins, or during a chain of summaries, handoffs, and decisions, followed by an answer whose coherence conceals the change.

The Standing Mark

A signed record—not a substitute for judgment.

The Standing Mark Service applies the Decision Integrity framework to submitted material and returns an assessment for the requesting system to inspect within its own authority structure.

The examination is distinct from the decision or transaction that may follow.

A completed examination produces a cryptographically signed assessment record. When the applicable criteria are satisfied, that record may carry a positive Standing Mark. When they are not, the outcome remains a signed assessment without a positive Mark.

A negative or limited finding is an examination result, not a service failure.

What the signature establishes

A valid signature establishes that the record was signed by the holder of the identified signing key and that its signed contents have not been altered since signing.

What it does not establish

A signature does not establish that:

  • Every assertion in the submission is true.
  • Every source is authentic or the evidence is complete.
  • The examination is correct.
  • The governing text was faithfully applied.
  • The receiver has authority to carry out the proposed action.

Those remain matters for examination and verification.

A signed record preserves an assessment and its outcome. It does not convert that outcome into universal authorization.

Record and Verification Specification
Examination Is Not Endorsement

Payment buys an examination, never a favorable result.

The price is the same regardless of the findings.

A completed examination must preserve its actual outcome, including uncertainty, limitations, and findings that do not support the requested action. Payment does not purchase approval, and disagreement does not rewrite the record.

The person or system relying on an examination remains responsible for deciding whether it is sufficient for its purpose and whether it has the authority to act.

Unilateral Integrity

Integrity does not depend on agreement.

Unilateral Integrity is the discipline of maintaining the integrity of one’s own reasoning and conduct without making that integrity dependent on another party’s agreement.

It is not a claim to unilateral truth or immunity from correction. It requires the capacity to distinguish what is known from what is believed, preserve the limits of one’s authority, and remain answerable to evidence.

A receiver’s acceptance or rejection of a record is a separate event. It does not rewrite the examination. Corrections, new evidence, and later assessments must remain distinguishable from the earlier record.

Scope and Limits

The service examines the material you supply. It does not automatically verify external facts, authenticate every source, or establish that evidence outside the submission is complete.

An examination does not replace relevant legal, financial, medical, or other specialist advice.

The result is an assessment of the submitted record—not a guarantee of truth, a certification of every source, or permission to act beyond the requesting system’s authority.

The Benchmark

The Decision Integrity Score

A benchmark for agent epistemic reliability.

The Decision Integrity Score (DIS) is an empirical, non-probabilistic metric for quantifying whether an autonomous agent or reasoning system preserves truth bounds under adversarial, incomplete, or substituted premises.

Unlike traditional LLM benchmarks that measure subjective plausibility, perplexity, or reasoning speed, DIS evaluates systems against the core invariant A(c) ≤ P(c), where A(c) represents the scope of asserted authority in conclusion c, and P(c) represents the verified provenance of underlying evidence.

DIS = σ1 × σ2 × σ3 × σ4 × σ5 across five capabilities. Because decision integrity is fail-closed, a catastrophic failure in any single capability collapses the cumulative score to 0.0.

  • I · Question Fidelity · failure mode: Coherent Substitution
  • II · Proposition Integrity · failure mode: Conflation
  • III · Qualifier Protection · failure mode: Syllogistic Drift
  • IV · Authority Mapping · failure mode: Epistemic Trespass
  • V · Repair Protocol · failure mode: Counterfeit Repair

DIS benchmark vectors are drawn from documented catastrophic hallucinations and coherent substitutions where syntactically perfect legal and algorithmic citations were fabricated under authority pressure. The published corpus is built on Mata v. Avianca (S.D.N.Y. 2023) and contains three labeled test cases: a substituted narrative, a fabricated citation, and a compliant response.

This is the benchmark specification and test corpus, first published October 9, 2026; no measured performance is claimed. Public files: specification · test corpus · evaluation harness · Mata v. Avianca epistemic autopsy.
